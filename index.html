Part 1: Foundational Challenges in Modern Physics

Contemporary physics faces a persistent theoretical impasse. For over a century, our understanding of the universe has been bifurcated into two successful but mathematically incompatible frameworks.

The Theoretical Divide

  • Quantum Mechanics: Describes the probabilistic behavior of energy and matter at microscopic scales.
  • General Relativity: Describes gravity as the geometric curvature of continuous spacetime at macroscopic scales.

Attempts to unify these frameworks typically result in mathematical singularities. Furthermore, observational cosmology has identified significant anomalies that the standard models cannot explain without introducing hypothetical, invisible components.

The Observational Anomalies: Galactic rotation curves indicate that visible matter is insufficient to generate the observed gravitational binding; this has necessitated the hypothesis of Dark Matter. Simultaneously, the accelerating expansion of the universe suggests a repulsive component, labeled Dark Energy. Together, these unseen sectors reportedly comprise roughly 95% of the universe's energy budget, yet direct detection remains elusive.

The Proposed Framework: This theory posits that these phenomena are not caused by exotic particles or fields, but are emergent artifacts of the information structure of spacetime itself.

Part 2: Spacetime as an Entanglement Network

This framework shifts the ontological baseline: rather than treating spacetime as a fundamental background, it treats it as an emergent network of quantum entanglement.

In this view, the "vacuum" corresponds to a state of maximal entanglement connectivity. Matter is not a distinct entity placed within this vacuum, but a topological defect—a region where the entanglement structure is locally suppressed or "constrained."

Mass as Information Deficit

m = \kappa_m \times S_{\text{ent}}

We calibrate this relationship by identifying the electron with a fundamental unit of information—exactly 1 bit of vacuum-subtracted entanglement entropy. This establishes $\kappa_m$ as a universal constant relating information (bits) to inertia (kg).

If mass is an informational deficit, gravity arises as the system's tendency to equilibrate. Spacetime curvature is the response of the entanglement network to the presence of these deficits.

  3. By calculating the geometric dilution of the entropic force from the Planck scale to the electron scale, we derive a theoretical value for gravitational coupling.

The resulting calculation yields the value of Newton's $G$ ($6.67 \times 10^{-11}$) directly from the mass of the electron and the speed of light, suggesting a fundamental unity between quantum mass and gravitational strength.

Part 4: Entropic Dynamics in Galaxies

This entropic description of gravity provides a natural resolution to the "Dark Matter" problem.

In massive systems like galaxies, the collective entanglement deficit creates a long-range gradient in the scalar field $S_{\text{ent}}$. This field generates an additional "entropic force" that manifests primarily in regions of low acceleration—the galactic outskirts.

  • The Entropic Halo: Instead of a halo of invisible particles, the galaxy is surrounded by a halo of information potential.
  • Predictive Power: This mechanism analytically reproduces the Baryonic Tully-Fisher Relation ($v^4 \propto M$), predicting the rotation velocities of galaxies based solely on their visible baryonic mass.

This suggests that the "missing mass" is a gravitational artifact of the vacuum's response to large-scale structure, rendering the Dark Matter particle hypothesis unnecessary.

Part 5: Cosmic Acceleration and Time Dilation

The theory addresses Dark Energy by examining the relationship between entanglement and time. In General Relativity, time is malleable; in this framework, the rate of temporal flow is governed by the local entanglement density.

Entropic Time Dilation: Cosmic voids—vast regions of empty space—are areas of maximal entanglement ($S_{\text{ent}} \approx S_{\text{max}}$). Consequently, proper time in voids progresses at a slightly faster rate relative to dense, matter-filled regions like our galactic neighborhood.

When observing distant supernovae (standard candles) across cosmic voids, this cumulative time dilation creates an apparent redshift anomaly. We perceive this as an accelerating expansion. Thus, cosmic acceleration is identified as a relativistic frame effect rather than the result of a negative-pressure fluid.

Part 6: The Many-Pasts Hypothesis

Finally, the framework necessitates a re-evaluation of cosmic history to preserve quantum unitarity. We propose the Many-Pasts Hypothesis.

Standard quantum mechanics treats the future as probabilistic. We extend this principle to the past. The history of the universe is treated as a quantum superposition of all possible timelines. The observable "Present" acts as a boundary condition, filtering this superposition for consistency.

  • Consistency Filter: Histories that do not mathematically converge to the current state are suppressed via destructive interference.
  • Entropic Selection: Among consistent histories, the path integral is weighted heavily toward timelines that maximize entanglement generation.

This provides a selection mechanism for the universe's initial conditions. The universe appears fine-tuned for complexity and structure because only those histories possess the requisite statistical weight to be realized. We are not accidental observers; we are the result of an evolutionary trajectory selected for maximum information complexity.
