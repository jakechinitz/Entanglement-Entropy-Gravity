Theory Overview

What is this theory about?

The central proposal is that gravity is not a separate, fundamental force but the macroscopic response of an underlying quantum-information medium. A single real scalar field $S_{\text{ent}}(x)$ measures how far each region of space is from a maximally entangled vacuum state. Wherever this field has a positive value, the vacuum is “missing” some entanglement links. Those missing links are interpreted as mass, and the way the vacuum rearranges itself around them shows up as spacetime curvature.

Part 1: Why a New Perspective on Gravity and the Cosmos?

Modern physics is anchored by two highly successful frameworks:

The Two Pillars

  • Quantum Mechanics: Governs the probabilistic behavior of particles, fields, and energy at microscopic scales.
  • General Relativity: Describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime produced by mass and energy at large scales.

Each theory works extremely well in its own regime. The difficulty appears when we try to apply both at once — near black holes, at the Big Bang, or at very small distances. Standard methods for combining quantum fields with curved spacetime often produce infinities or contradictions.

At the same time, our current cosmological model leans heavily on two invisible ingredients:

Dark Matter: added to explain why galaxies rotate as if they contain far more mass than we can see in stars and gas.
Dark Energy: introduced to explain why the expansion of the universe appears to be accelerating rather than slowing down.

Together, these unseen components are claimed to make up about 95% of the universe’s energy content. Yet, despite decades of searches, we have no direct non-gravitational evidence for what they are.

This program explores a different possibility: that gravity, dark-matter–like behavior, and dark-energy–like behavior are not separate substances, but emergent consequences of how quantum information is arranged and redistributed in spacetime.

What problems is it trying to solve?

The six papers are organized around a set of linked questions:

Target Problems

  • Why is $G$ what it is? In standard physics, Newton’s constant is a measured input. Here, $G$ is derived from an information density at the Planck scale, a mass–information constant $\kappa_m$, and a single geometric sharing factor $g_{\text{share}}$.
  • Galaxy rotation & the RAR: Flat rotation curves and the radial acceleration relation are reproduced without dark matter particles, via an entropic response of the vacuum encoded by $S_{\text{ent}}$.
  • Lepton mass pattern: The electron, muon, and tau masses follow a regular log-quadratic pattern. The curvature of that pattern is tied, in this framework, to the same microscopic constant $g_{\text{share}}$.
  • Cosmic acceleration: The apparent speed-up in cosmic expansion is reinterpreted in terms of entanglement-controlled time dilation between voids and matter-rich regions, rather than a new dark energy fluid.

Part 2: The Universe as an Entanglement Network

The framework starts from a shift in how we view the vacuum. Instead of “empty space,” we imagine an underlying web of quantum correlations connecting every region of space to every other. In physics, these connections are called entanglement.

In this picture, a perfectly “empty” vacuum is not the absence of structure, but the state where this web is as richly and evenly connected as possible. Geometry — distances, angles, and curvature — is the large-scale appearance of how this web is arranged.

Matter is then reinterpreted. An electron, for example, is not a tiny billiard ball sitting inside space. It is a small region where the usual entanglement links have been disrupted or removed. The web is less connected there.

Mass as Entanglement Deficit

This leads to a new definition: a particle’s mass is proportional to how much entanglement is missing compared to the vacuum. The field $S_{\text{ent}}(x)$ measures this “entanglement deficit” at each point in space.

$$m = \kappa_m \times S_{\text{ent}}$$

Here, $S_{\text{ent}}$ is measured in bits and $m$ in kilograms. The constant $\kappa_m$ converts “missing information” into ordinary inertial mass.

A key calibration in this program is: a single free electron corresponds to exactly 1 bit of vacuum-subtracted entanglement entropy at its natural length scale. Once that one-bit anchor is imposed, $\kappa_m$ is fixed, and every other mass and gravitational effect must be consistent with that choice.

Part 3: Deriving Gravity from Information

Once mass is defined as an entanglement deficit, gravity can be viewed as the vacuum’s attempt to smooth out those deficits. Regions with more missing entanglement pull more strongly on the surrounding web as the system tries to rebalance its connections. At large scales, this rebalancing looks like the familiar attraction between massive bodies and the bending of spacetime.

Technically, the theory is formulated as an effective field theory for the scalar field $S_{\text{ent}}(x)$. Gradients and time-variations of this field contribute to the stress–energy that curves spacetime, modifying Einstein’s equations in a controlled way.

A central test of this picture is whether it can explain the strength of gravity, encoded in Newton’s constant $G$, without simply measuring it and inserting the number by hand.

First-Principles Derivation of $G$

  1. Begin with a Planck-scale anchor: interpret the maximum “tension” of the vacuum as an information density at the smallest meaningful scale.
  2. Introduce a geometric sharing factor $g_{\text{share}} \approx 7.4$, which counts how many independent “channels” the vacuum uses to redistribute the entanglement deficit created by a particle. In the more microscopic model, this constant is tied to a spin-3 condensate and comes out close to $\ln(1680)$, a pure number with no units.
  3. Impose the electron calibration: at the electron’s length scale, a single electron carries exactly 1 bit of entanglement deficit, so $\kappa_m$ must reproduce the known electron mass.

Following how the entanglement deficit spreads from the Planck scale out to the electron scale, and accounting for the geometric sharing encoded in $g_{\text{share}}$, yields a theoretical value for Newton’s $G$ that matches the observed $6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}^3\text{kg}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$. In this framework, the strength of gravity is set by how efficiently the vacuum can distribute one bit of missing entanglement around a particle.

Part 4: Entropic Dynamics in Galaxies

The same field $S_{\text{ent}}$ that defines mass also extends over large distances. In a galaxy, the entanglement deficits from billions of stars and gas clouds do not stay sharply localized. They spread out and form a smooth, long-range gradient.

This gradient behaves like an extra gravitational potential. Not because there is additional unseen matter, but because the vacuum’s entanglement structure has been distorted on galactic scales. The result is an entropic halo surrounding the visible galaxy.

  • The Entropic Halo: Instead of a halo of invisible particles, the galaxy is embedded in a halo of elevated entanglement deficit — an “information potential” generated by the vacuum’s response to the visible mass.
  • Predictive Power: In the outer parts of galaxies, where the usual Newtonian pull from visible matter is very weak, this entropic contribution becomes dominant. The framework analytically reproduces the Baryonic Tully–Fisher Relation ($v^4 \propto M$), connecting a galaxy’s rotation speed to its visible baryonic mass.

From this point of view, the “missing mass” problem is reframed. The extra gravitational pull we attribute to dark matter arises from the way the entanglement field responds to large-scale structure, not from a new population of unseen particles.

Part 5: Cosmic Acceleration and Time Dilation

General Relativity already tells us that gravity affects time: clocks run more slowly in stronger gravitational fields. This framework extends that idea and ties the local “speed of time” to the local entanglement structure of the vacuum.

Regions that are closer to the maximally entangled vacuum — with fewer matter-induced deficits — are proposed to experience slightly faster proper time. Cosmic voids, the enormous underdense regions between galaxy clusters, are then places where $S_{\text{ent}}$ is closest to its vacuum value and entanglement is most complete.

Denser regions, like galaxies and clusters, contain more entanglement deficits and therefore run slightly “slower” in this entropic sense. When light from distant supernovae travels to us, it passes through large stretches of high-entanglement voids. Over cosmological distances, even tiny differences in the local rate of time can accumulate into a noticeable effect.

In this interpretation, at least part of the observed cosmic acceleration can be understood as a large-scale time-dilation pattern generated by the entanglement structure of the vacuum. What we currently model as dark energy may be, in part, a relativistic frame effect produced by entropic time dilation across the cosmic web.

Part 6: Many Pasts and Entropic Selection

The final piece of the program asks how far an information-based view can be pushed. Standard quantum mechanics treats the future as a branching set of possibilities but usually treats the past as fixed. This framework explores what happens if we relax that assumption and treat entire spacetime histories as quantum objects.

The Many-Pasts Hypothesis views the universe’s history as a superposition of many possible pasts and futures, all of which must be consistent with the present state we observe.

  • Consistency Filter: Histories that cannot lead to the observed present, once all physical constraints are enforced, interfere destructively and effectively drop out.
  • Entropic Selection: Among the remaining consistent histories, those that generate more entanglement — more complex, richly structured universes — are given much higher weight.

This provides a possible explanation for why the universe appears so “well-tuned” for structure, chemistry, and life. Histories that fizzle out into featureless simplicity generate little entanglement and contribute weakly. Histories that develop galaxies, stars, molecules, and eventually observers like us generate vast amounts of entanglement and dominate the quantum weighting.
