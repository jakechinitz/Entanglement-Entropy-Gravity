Part 1: Why a New Perspective on Gravity and the Cosmos?

Modern physics rests on two extraordinarily successful theories that describe different parts of reality:

The Two Pillars

  • Quantum Mechanics: Governs the probabilistic behavior of particles, fields, and energy at microscopic scales.
  • General Relativity: Describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy at large scales.

Each framework has passed every experimental test in its own domain. The difficulty arises when we try to combine them. At very high energies, very small distances, or near black holes and the Big Bang, the standard tools for merging quantum theory with curved spacetime tend to produce infinities and inconsistencies.

At the same time, our best cosmological model requires adding two invisible components:

Dark Matter: Introduced to explain why galaxies rotate as if they contain far more mass than the stars and gas we can see.
Dark Energy: Invoked to explain why the expansion of the universe is accelerating rather than slowing down.

Together, these unseen sectors are said to make up about 95% of the universe’s total energy budget. Yet, despite decades of effort, we have no direct non-gravitational evidence for what they actually are.

The Proposed Framework: Instead of postulating new substances, this program explores the idea that gravity, dark matter effects, and dark energy–like behavior are emergent consequences of how quantum information is distributed and rearranged in spacetime.

Part 2: Spacetime as an Entanglement Network

The key shift is conceptual. Rather than treating spacetime as a smooth, fundamental background, we treat it as the large-scale appearance of something more microscopic: an underlying network of quantum entanglement.

In this view, the “vacuum” is not empty. It is a state where the underlying degrees of freedom are maximally interconnected. Every region of space is entangled, in a subtle way, with every other region. This web of correlations is the substrate from which spacetime and geometry emerge.

Matter, in this picture, is not “stuff placed into” the vacuum. Instead, matter corresponds to local disruptions of this entanglement network — tiny regions where some of the possible connections are missing or constrained. You can think of these as topological defects in the underlying information structure.

Mass as Information Deficit

This leads to a new way of defining mass. If the vacuum is the state of maximal entanglement, then a particle is a local reduction in that entanglement. We call this reduction the entanglement deficit. Mass becomes a measure of how much entanglement is missing compared to the vacuum.

m = \kappa_m \times S_{\text{ent}}

Here, $S_{\text{ent}}$ is a scalar field that counts the entanglement deficit in units of information (bits), and $m$ is the usual inertial mass in kilograms. The constant $\kappa_m$ converts between “missing entanglement” and “mass.”

A central calibration in this framework is the statement that the electron corresponds to exactly 1 bit of vacuum-subtracted entanglement entropy at its natural length scale. Once that identification is made, $\kappa_m$ is fixed. From that point on, every mass and every gravitational effect linked to mass must emerge from the same information-to-mass conversion factor.

Part 3: The Emergence of Gravitation

If mass is an entanglement deficit, gravity no longer needs to be introduced as a separate, unexplained force. Instead, it can be understood as the large-scale response of the entanglement network as it tries to smooth out those deficits.

Intuitively, regions of space with more missing entanglement “pull” on the surrounding network. The system rearranges its connections to reduce imbalances, and this rearrangement shows up, at macroscopic scales, as the bending of spacetime and the familiar attraction we call gravity.

In the technical papers, this is implemented as an effective field theory: the scalar field $S_{\text{ent}}(x)$ is allowed to vary across spacetime, and its gradients contribute to the stress–energy that curves spacetime, modifying Einstein’s equations in a controlled way.

A key test of this picture is whether it can explain the strength of gravity, encoded in Newton’s gravitational constant $G$, without simply inserting it by hand.

First-Principles Derivation of $G$

  1. We start from a Planck-scale anchor, interpreting the maximum vacuum “tension” as an information density at the smallest meaningful scale.
  2. We introduce a geometric sharing factor, $g_{\text{share}} \approx 7.43$, which counts how many independent “channels” the vacuum uses to redistribute entanglement around a disturbance. In the full theory, this number is tied to the combinatorics of a specific quantum medium and is closely related to $\ln(1680)$, a pure, dimensionless constant.
  3. We impose the electron calibration: at the electron’s natural length scale, one electron carries exactly 1 bit of entanglement deficit, so $\kappa_m$ must reproduce the known electron mass at that scale.

When these ingredients are combined and the entanglement deficit is followed from the Planck scale out to the electron scale, the resulting calculation yields a value of Newton’s $G$ that matches the observed $6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\text{m}^3\text{kg}^{-1}\text{s}^{-2}$ to current precision. In this framework, the strength of gravity is not arbitrary; it is set by how efficiently the quantum vacuum can share and redistribute one bit of missing entanglement.

Part 4: Entropic Dynamics in Galaxies

The same scalar field $S_{\text{ent}}$ that defines mass also extends over large distances. In a galaxy, the entanglement deficits from billions of stars and clouds of gas do not remain sharply localized. They spread out and generate a smooth, long-range gradient in $S_{\text{ent}}$.

This gradient behaves like an additional gravitational potential. Not because there is extra invisible matter, but because the vacuum’s entanglement structure has been distorted on galactic scales. The result is an entropic halo surrounding the visible galaxy.

  • The Entropic Halo: Instead of a halo of unseen particles, the galaxy is embedded in a halo of elevated entanglement deficit — an “information potential” that arises from the vacuum’s response to the visible mass.
  • Predictive Power: In the outer parts of galaxies, where ordinary Newtonian gravity from visible matter is very weak, this entropic contribution becomes dominant. The theory analytically reproduces the Baryonic Tully–Fisher Relation ($v^4 \propto M$), linking a galaxy’s rotation speed to its visible baryonic mass.

In this picture, the apparent “missing mass” is not a separate dark substance. It is a sign that the vacuum’s information structure is participating in the dynamics. The extra gravitational pull we attribute to Dark Matter emerges from how the entanglement field responds to large-scale structure.

Part 5: Cosmic Acceleration and Time Dilation

General Relativity already tells us that time does not flow at the same rate everywhere: clocks tick more slowly in stronger gravitational fields. This framework extends that insight and ties the local “speed of time” to the local entanglement structure of the vacuum.

Entropic Time Dilation: Regions of space that are closer to the maximally entangled vacuum — with fewer matter-induced defects — are proposed to experience slightly faster proper time. Cosmic voids, the enormous underdense regions between galaxy clusters, are then places where $S_{\text{ent}}$ is near its minimal (vacuum) value and entanglement is closest to maximal.

Denser regions, like galaxies and clusters, contain more entanglement deficits and therefore run slightly “slower” in this entropic sense. When light from distant supernovae and galaxies travels to us, much of its path passes through these high-entanglement voids. Over cosmological distances, even tiny differences in the local rate of time can accumulate.

In this interpretation, at least part of the observed cosmic acceleration can be understood as a large-scale time-dilation pattern generated by the entanglement structure of the vacuum. What we currently model as “Dark Energy” — a mysterious fluid with negative pressure — may instead be, in part, a relativistic frame effect arising from entropic time dilation across the cosmic web.

Part 6: The Many-Pasts Hypothesis

Finally, the program explores what happens when we apply information-based thinking not just to space and time, but to history itself.

Standard quantum mechanics treats the future as a set of possibilities and assigns probabilities to different outcomes. The past, by contrast, is usually treated as fixed: there was one definite history, and our job is to reconstruct it.

The Many-Pasts Hypothesis relaxes that assumption. It treats the entire spacetime history of the universe as a quantum object — a superposition of many possible pasts and futures, all of which must be consistent with the present we observe.

  • Consistency Filter: Histories that cannot evolve into our current universe, once all physical constraints are enforced, interfere destructively and effectively drop out of consideration.
  • Entropic Selection: Among the remaining consistent histories, those that generate more entanglement — more complex, richly connected structures — are given much higher weight in the quantum path integral.

This provides a possible explanation for why the universe appears “fine-tuned” for structure, chemistry, and life. We observe a cosmos that is exceptionally good at generating complexity because histories that fail to do so contribute very little to the present state. In this sense, we are not improbable accidents in an otherwise sterile universe; we are typical observers in a history that has been statistically selected for high information content.

Summary

The Entanglement–Entropy Gravity program proposes a unified physical picture:

  • Spacetime emerges from an underlying network of quantum entanglement.
  • Mass is proportional to an entanglement deficit: missing connections relative to the vacuum.
  • Gravity is the entropic response of the vacuum as it redistributes and smooths those deficits.
  • Dark Matter effects arise from large-scale gradients in the entanglement field, not unseen particles.
  • Dark Energy–like behavior can emerge from entanglement-driven time dilation across the cosmic web.
  • Cosmic history is biased toward paths that maximize entanglement and information complexity.

The technical papers linked on this site attempt to make this picture concrete: the same information-based constants are used to derive the strength of gravity, explain galaxy-scale regularities, and connect to the observed mass pattern of elementary particles. The goal is not to declare the final theory of everything, but to offer a coherent, testable alternative in which “matter,” “gravity,” and “dark sectors” are different faces of a deeper quantity: the organization of quantum information itself.
