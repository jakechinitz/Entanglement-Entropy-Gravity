PURE CONDENSATE

Entanglement, Information, and Gravity

A six-paper research program exploring whether mass, gravity, dark matter, and dark energy can all be understood as consequences of how quantum information is arranged in space and time.

Key Program Claim

This work proposes that a single information-theoretic quantity — the entanglement deficit of the quantum vacuum — can simultaneously:

  • define what mass is, in units of information (bits),
  • generate gravity as an emergent, entropic effect,
  • explain galaxy rotation curves without dark matter particles,
  • account for cosmic acceleration without a mysterious dark-energy fluid, and
  • relate these large-scale phenomena to the observed masses of the electron, muon, and tau.

In other words: the same microscopic information structure that gives a particle its mass also sets the strength of gravity and shapes the large-scale behavior of the universe.

Abstract (Informal)

The central idea is simple to state: the vacuum of space is not “empty,” but a densely connected web of quantum entanglement. Ordinary matter corresponds to tiny disruptions in that web — places where some of the possible connections are missing. These disruptions are measured by a scalar field, denoted Sent(x), which counts how much entanglement has been removed in a given region.

This program takes that idea seriously and pushes it as far as possible. It treats Sent(x) as the basic ingredient of a new effective field theory and asks: if we assume that mass is proportional to this entanglement deficit, and we let the deficit spread and equilibrate in a mathematically consistent way, do we recover anything like the real universe? The six papers linked on this site argue that the answer is “yes” to a surprising extent.

The theory claims to derive Newton’s gravitational constant from information-theoretic inputs, to reproduce key galaxy-scale regularities that are usually attributed to dark matter, and to offer a new perspective on cosmic acceleration and the apparent fine-tuning of the universe’s history — all from the behavior of a single scalar “entanglement field.”

Layperson Overview

Part 1: Why We Need a New Perspective

Modern physics is built on two exceptionally successful theories that describe different domains of reality:

The Two Great Frameworks

  • Quantum Mechanics: governs particles, fields, and probabilities at microscopic scales.
  • General Relativity: describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime at large scales.

Each of these theories has passed every experimental test in its own regime. The problem is that they do not seamlessly fit together. When we try to combine them in extreme situations — near black holes, at the Big Bang, or at very small distances — the usual mathematical tools produce infinities and contradictions.

At the same time, our best cosmological model depends heavily on two invisible components:

Dark Matter: introduced to explain why galaxies rotate as if they contain far more mass than we can see.
Dark Energy: invoked to explain why the expansion of the universe is accelerating rather than slowing down.

Together, these unseen entities are said to make up about 95% of the universe’s energy content. Yet, after decades of experiments, we have no direct non-gravitational evidence for what they actually are.

This research program starts from a different angle. Instead of adding more ingredients (new particles, new mysterious fluids), it asks:

What if gravity, dark matter, and dark energy are not separate substances at all,
but different manifestations of how information is stored and redistributed in the fabric of quantum spacetime?

Part 2: Spacetime as an Entanglement Network

The key shift is conceptual. Rather than taking “spacetime” as a smooth stage on which physics happens, we treat it as the large-scale appearance of something more microscopic: an underlying network of quantum entanglement.

In this view, what we normally call the “vacuum” is not empty. It is the state where this network is as richly interconnected as possible — a sort of maximally tangled background of correlations between every region of space and every other.

Matter, in this picture, is not something placed into that vacuum. Instead, matter is a pattern of missing connections: a local disruption of the network. You can think of it as a tiny region where some of the usual entanglement links have been removed or constrained. The theory calls this a topological defect in the entanglement structure.

Mass as “Entanglement Deficit”

This leads to a simple but powerful redefinition: mass measures how much entanglement is missing compared to the vacuum. The more you deform the entanglement network in a region, the more “massive” that region appears.

To make this precise, we introduce a scalar field:

Sent(x) = “how much entanglement is missing here?”

and we define mass via a proportionality:

m = \kappa_m \times S_{\text{ent}}.

Here, Sent is measured in units of information (bits), and m is measured in kilograms. The constant \kappa_m converts “missing entanglement” into ordinary inertial mass.

A central calibration in this program is: the electron corresponds to exactly 1 bit of vacuum-subtracted entanglement. That statement fixes the numerical value of \kappa_m once and for all. From that point on, every mass in the theory, and every gravitational effect linked to mass, must emerge from the same information-to-mass conversion factor.

Part 3: How Gravity Emerges from Information

Once mass is reinterpreted as an entanglement deficit, gravity no longer needs to be treated as a separate fundamental force. Instead, it appears as a collective response of the entanglement network trying to smooth out those deficits.

Intuitively:

Entanglement wants to “even out.”
If some region of space has less entanglement than its surroundings (because matter is present), the network rearranges itself. That rearrangement shows up, at large scales, as the bending of spacetime and the familiar pull of gravity.

Mathematically, this is encoded in an effective field theory where the scalar field Sent(x) is allowed to vary in space and time, and its gradients contribute to the stress–energy that curves spacetime. The resulting equations look very similar to Einstein’s equations, but with an extra “entropic” contribution coming from the information structure of the vacuum.

Deriving Newton’s Constant from Microscopic Inputs

In standard physics, Newton’s gravitational constant G is an experimentally measured number. We insert it into our equations because it works, but we do not explain where its value comes from.

In this framework, the goal is to compute G from more basic ingredients. The derivation uses three pieces:

  1. A Planck-scale anchor: the maximum possible “tension” of the vacuum, interpreted as an information density at the smallest meaningful scale.
  2. A geometric sharing factor: gshare \approx 7.43, which counts how many independent “channels” the vacuum uses to distribute entanglement around a disturbance. In the more technical papers, this constant is tied to the combinatorics of a particular spin-3 quantum medium and turns out to be very close to ln(1680), a pure number with no units.
  3. The electron calibration: the requirement that a single electron corresponds to exactly one bit of entanglement deficit at its natural length scale.

When these ingredients are combined and followed through the equations, the theory produces a value of G that matches the observed gravitational constant to within current experimental precision — using only information-theoretic assumptions plus known constants like the electron mass and the speed of light.

In short: gravity’s strength is not arbitrary here. It is set by how efficiently the quantum vacuum can redistribute entanglement around a one-bit defect.

Part 4: Galaxies Without Dark Matter Particles

The same scalar field Sent that defines mass also evolves over large distances. In extended systems like galaxies, the entanglement deficit created by all the stars and gas does not remain sharply localized. It spreads out and forms a smooth, long-range gradient.

This gradient acts like an additional gravitational potential — not because there is extra invisible mass, but because the vacuum’s entanglement structure is distorted on large scales. The theory describes this as an entropic halo: a region surrounding the visible galaxy where Sent is elevated above its vacuum value, even though there are no new particles present.

In the outer parts of galaxies, where the usual Newtonian acceleration from visible matter is very small, this entropic contribution becomes dominant. The result is:

  • Flat or gently rising rotation curves, matching what astronomers observe without needing a dark matter halo made of unknown particles.
  • An analytic prediction of the Baryonic Tully–Fisher Relation, which connects a galaxy’s visible mass to the fourth power of its rotation speed (v⁴ ∝ M). In this framework, that scaling emerges naturally from how the entanglement field responds to a given amount of baryonic (ordinary) mass.

From this point of view, the “missing mass” problem is reframed. The discrepancy between visible matter and observed gravity is not evidence of a hidden particle population, but a sign that the vacuum’s information structure is participating in the dynamics in a way that standard gravity equations do not yet account for.

Part 5: Time, Cosmic Acceleration, and the Role of the Vacuum

General Relativity already tells us that time does not flow at the same rate everywhere: clocks tick more slowly in strong gravitational fields. This theory extends that idea to the information domain.

The proposal is that the local rate of time is influenced by the local entanglement density of the vacuum. Regions with more “pure” vacuum (fewer defects, fewer constraints) are closer to maximal entanglement and experience slightly faster proper time than regions where the vacuum is heavily disturbed.

Cosmic voids — enormous regions with very little matter — are then places where the vacuum is closest to its maximally entangled state. In contrast, dense environments (galaxies, clusters) have more entanglement deficits and slightly slower local time.

When light from distant supernovae and galaxies travels to us, much of its path goes through these high-entanglement voids. If time in those regions runs just a bit faster, the accumulated effect can mimic what we currently interpret as an accelerating cosmic expansion.

In this interpretation, at least part of “dark energy” is not a new substance with negative pressure, but a subtle, large-scale time-dilation pattern created by the entanglement structure of the vacuum itself.

Part 6: Many Pasts and the Selection of Histories

Finally, the program explores what happens when we apply information-theoretic thinking not just to space and time, but to history itself.

Standard quantum mechanics treats the future as a set of possibilities and uses probabilities to weigh them. The past, by contrast, is usually treated as fixed: there was one precise history, and we are trying to discover it.

The Many-Pasts Hypothesis challenges that separation. It treats the entire space–time history of the universe as a quantum object — a superposition of many possible pasts and futures, all of which must be consistent with the present we observe.

In that picture:

  • Histories that cannot lead to our current universe, when all constraints are applied, interfere destructively and effectively drop out.
  • Among the remaining consistent histories, the ones that generate more entanglement — more complex structure, more richly connected states — are given much higher weight.

This provides a possible explanation for the apparent fine-tuning of the universe. We observe a cosmos that is unusually good at forming galaxies, stars, chemistry, and life because histories that failed to generate such complexity simply do not contribute much to the present state. The universe looks “designed” for information growth not because of an external designer, but because quantum dynamics strongly favors entanglement-rich histories over entanglement-poor ones.

Summary

The Entanglement–Entropy Gravity program develops a single, consistent story:

  • Spacetime is the large-scale appearance of an underlying entanglement network.
  • Mass is proportional to how much entanglement is missing (the entanglement deficit).
  • Gravity is the vacuum’s entropic response to those deficits.
  • Dark Matter effects arise from long-range gradients in the entanglement field, not unseen particles.
  • Dark Energy–like behavior emerges from variations in the entanglement-controlled rate of time.
  • Cosmic history is biased toward paths that maximize entanglement and information complexity.

The technical papers connected to this site attempt to show that this story is not just philosophically appealing, but mathematically concrete: the same information-based constants can be used to derive the strength of gravity, explain galaxy-scale regularities, and connect to the observed mass pattern of fundamental particles.

Whether this framework is ultimately correct is an open question. The intention of this work is to present a coherent, testable alternative: a universe where what we call “matter” and “gravity” are different faces of a deeper quantity — the organization of quantum information itself.

The Six-Paper Program

The detailed arguments are distributed across six papers:

  1. Entanglement Entropy: Unifying the Quantum Origins of Gravity, Mass, Time, and Cosmic Structure – defines $S_{\\text{ent}}(x)$, $\\kappa_m$, and the broad emergent picture.
  2. Supplement: Group Field / Tensor Underpinnings of the Entropic Scalar EFT – builds the discrete microstructure and coarse-graining flow.
  3. Derivations and Numerical Validations for the Entropic Scalar EFT – pins down the action, units, and numerics for $\\kappa_m$ and galactic scaling.
  4. Theoretical Foundations of the Wedge-Sharing Constant $g_{\\text{share}}$ – geometric derivation and random-walk persistence bounds.
  5. Entanglement–Scalar Derivation of $G$ (De Novo $G$) – shows how $G$ is fixed once $g_{\\text{share}}$ is specified.
  6. Microstructure & Phenomenological Supplement – RAR / $a_0$ derivation, lepton shell Hamiltonian, Hawking-like checks, and spin-3 condensate consistency.
